\section{Asymmetric intersection number and non-examples}
\label{sec:asymmetric}
One might wonder whether Theorem~\ref{thm:intersect} applies to
\emph{oriented} currents with an appropriate asymmetric notion of intersection.
Indeed, up to Proposition~\ref{prop:recoverintersection}, we managed
to construct a geodesic current~$\mu_f$ associated to a
not-necessarily-symmetric curve functional~$f$. One could hope that
(asymmetric) intersections with $\mu_f$ recover $f$ in the more
general context. In this section, we dash this hope: there are
asymmetric curve functionals satisfying oriented \DMGtodo{added oriented here, I think we need it?} smoothing and additivity that
are not realized as asymmetric intersection numbers with any oriented
geodesic currents.

The asymmetric intersection number is an intersection number between oriented curves, defined analogously as the geometric intersection number but only considering right-handed intersections (Definition~\ref{def:crossing}).

\begin{definition} Given two oriented curves $\vec C$, $\vec D$, the
  asymmetric intersection number $\vec i(\vec C, \vec D)$ is the
  minimal number of right-handed crossings between any two homotopy
  class representatives of $C$ and~$D$.

  Likewise define $h(\vec C)\in H_1(\Sigma)$ to be the homology class
  associated to~$\vec C$, and let 
  $\langle h(\vec C), h(\vec D)\rangle$ be the algebraic intersection
  number, the number of right-handed crossings minus the number of
  left-handed crossings in any generic representatives of $\vec C$
  and~$\vec D$~\cite[6.1]{FM12:PrimerMCG}.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:asym-intersect}
  If $\langle \vec C, \vec D\rangle$ is the algebraic intersection
  number between the (homology classes of) $\vec C$ and $\vec D$, then
  \[
    \vec i(\vec C, \vec D) = i(C, D) + {\textstyle\frac12}\langle \vec C,\vec D\rangle.
  \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate from the definitions.
\end{proof}

As a result, we have strong constraints on which asymmetric curve
functionals can come from an asymmetric intersection number.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:asym-constraint}
  For any collection of oriented curves $\{\vec C_i\}_{i=1}^k$ so that
  $\sum_i h(\vec C_i) = 0$, let $\vec{\mathbf{C}} = \bigcup_i (\vec C_i
  \cup -\vec C_i)$. Then for any oriented curve~$\vec D$, we have
  \[
    \vec i(\vec{\mathbf C},D) = 0.
  \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate from Lemma~\ref{lem:asym-intersect}.
\end{proof}

It is now easy to construct asymmetric functionals that cannot come
from intersection numbers. First consider the flat unpunctured torus,
and, consider the functional $f$ on given by word-length with respect
to a monoid generating set corresponding to an oriented triangle. With
respect to standard generators $a,b$ with $[a,b] = e$, we consider the
three (monoid) generators $a$, $b$, and $c = a^{-1}b^{-1}$. Word length with \DMGtodo{why is it clear that this satisfies oriented smoothing?}

In particular, consider the three curves
\begin{align*}
  \vec C_1 &= \overrightarrow{[a]} & 
  \vec C_2 &= \overrightarrow{[b]} & 
  \vec C_3 &= \overrightarrow{[c]}.
\end{align*}
These satisfy the conditions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:asym-constraint}, but have lengths
$\ell(\vec C_i) = 1$ and $\ell(-\vec C_i) = 2$. Concretely,
Proposition~\ref{prop:asym-constraint} says that the alternating sum
of lengths of curves around the central hexagon must be zero, as you
can see by example in Figure~\ref{fig:shortcurves}.

 Develop these simple curves in the universal cover of the triangular punctured torus, and consider the same triangulation as in Figure~\ref{fig:shortcurves} lifted to the universal cover.

 The corresponding lifts of geodesics intersect the edges of the triangulations, which we orient. If we note the asymmetric intersection number of these geodesics as we develop the torus further and further, which corresponds to taking more powers of the curve, and keep track of the right-handed intersections, we obtain the diagrams of asymmetric intersections for each curve, color coded in Figure~\ref{fig:shortcurves}. We observe that counting right-handed crossings corresponds to taking the maximum of the algebraic intersection number, a linear function on simple curves, with 0.
