\section{Crossings}
\label{sec:crossings}

\subsection{Left vs Right actions of $\pi_1(S)$}
\label{sec:actions}

To fix conventions, we briefly recall elementary facts about actions of $\pi_1(S,\ast)$ on the universal
cover~$\wt S$ of a surface (or indeed any connected length space). We
follow the convention that path composition works in the natural
order, where a product $\gamma_1 \cdot \gamma_2$ of paths in~$S$ is
defined if $\gamma_1(1) = \gamma_2(0)$.
If $a \in \pi_1(S,\ast)$, we let $[a]$ denote the free homotopy class of $a$.
In particular, we note that $[g a g^{-1}]=[a]$.

\begin{definition}[Right action]
For a covering map $p \colon \wt S \to S$ of~$S$ and base-point
$\ast \in S$, we then have a \emph{right} action of $\pi_1(S,\ast)$ on
the fiber $p^{-1}(\ast)$, defined by, for $\bm{x} \in p^{-1}(\ast)$,
$\bm{x}\cdot[\gamma] = \wt \gamma(1)$, where $\wt\gamma$ is the
unique lift of~$\gamma$ with $\wt\gamma(0) = \bm{x}$.
The bold font denotes points in $\wt{S}$.
We observe
the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item This right action does not depend on a choice of basepoint
  on~$\wt S$.
\item In the induced length metric on~$\wt S$, the distance between
  $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{x}\cdot [\gamma]$ is bounded by the length of~$\gamma$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Left action]
When $\wt S$ is the universal cover of~$S$ (or, more generally, any
normal cover), there is also a \emph{left} action of $\pi_1(S,\ast)$
on all of $\wt S$ by deck transformations. If you take
the standard model of $\wt S$ as homotopy classes of paths~$\gamma$
in~$S$ from $\ast$ (with homotopies fixing the endpoints), then this
action is defined by
\DPTtodo{Is this notation for points in $\wt S$ too obscure? Yes, add
  some letter}
$[\gamma_1] \cdot \bm{[\gamma_2]} \coloneqq
\bm{[\gamma_1\cdot\gamma_2]}$. Here we make the following observations.
\begin{itemize}
\item This left action is by an isometry of all of~$\wt S$.
\item The left action depends on a lift of
  $\ast \in S$ to a basepoint  $\wt \ast \in \wt S$, for instance
  since the left and right actions are
  related by $[\gamma] \cdot \wt \ast = \wt \ast \cdot [\gamma]$.
\item The distance between $\bm{x}$ and $[\gamma] \cdot \bm{x}$
  may be unbounded, although the translation length of $[\gamma]$ on
  $\wt S$ is bounded by the length of $\gamma$ (by the previous bullet point).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

We will frequently draw pictures of group elements~$g\in\pi_1(S,\ast)$
acting on~$\H^2$. In these pictures, the arrows will be paths that
are lifts of~$g$, and thus correspond to the \emph{right} action on the
fiber.

In our constructions, we will use a choice of closed curve~$[x]$ and
different lifts of it to~$\wt S$.%
\footnote{In the main construction $[x]$ is a simple curve, although
  we will sometimes work with non-simple curves.} %
For convenience, we will work in $\pi_1(S,\ast)$ with a
basepoint~$\ast$ on the curve~$[x]$ and use $x$ for the corresponding
element of~$\pi_1$. There is then a base lift $\wt x$ of~$x$ to
$\wt S$, the lift that passes through the base point~$\wt *$ and all
elements of the group $\langle x \rangle$ generated by~$x$. Every
other lift $q \cdot \wt x$ of~$x$ passes through the points of a left
coset $q\langle x\rangle$ of this subgroup. Given two different lifts
$\ell_1,\ell_2$, passing through cosets $q_1 \langle x \rangle$ and
$q_2 \langle x \rangle$ respectively, they are related by the isometry
\begin{equation}
  \ell_2 = q_2 q_1^{-1} \cdot \ell_1
\end{equation}
but the geometric relation between the two lifts (up to the left
$\pi_1(S,*)$ action) is captured instead by the double coset
\begin{equation}\label{eq:double_coset}
  \langle x \rangle q_1^{-1} q_2 \langle x \rangle.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Parallelism and crossing of hyperbolic axes}

Let $\Isom^+(\mathbb{H}^2)$ denote the group of oriented  isometries of the hyperbolic plane. Given $a \in \Isom^+(\mathbb{H}^2)$ a hyperbolic isometry, its axis will be denoted $\ora{a}$, with attractive fixed points at the boundary at infinity $a^+, a^-$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A pair $(\ora{a},\ora{b})$ \emph{cross} if $b^+, a^-, b^-, a^+$ appear in this counter-clockwise order on the boundary at infinity. See Figure~\ref{fig:cross}.
    \item A pair $(\ora{a},\ora{b})$ is \emph{parallel} if $b^+, a^+, a^-, b^-$ appear in this counter-clockwise order on the boundary at infinity. See Figure~\ref{fig:parallel}.
   \item  A pair $(\ora{a},\ora{b})$ is \emph{anti-parallel} if $b^-, a^+, a^-, b^+$ appear in this counter-clockwise order on the boundary at infinity. See Figure~\ref{fig:anti}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Right-handed crossing and parallelism}

Now we connect the definition of right-handed crossing to the notions of crossing and parallelism of hyperbolic isometries.
If we introduce a hyperbolic metric $\Sigma$ on $S$, this yields a
homomorphism $\rho \colon \pi_1(S) \to \Isom^{+}(\mathbb{H}^2)$, so
that any non-trivial $a \in \pi_1(S)$ can be  identified with a
hyperbolic isometry $\rho(a) \in \Isom^+(\mathbb{H}^2)$.
% \DMGtodo[inline]{(Punctures) If punctures, we need to ask for
%   hyperbolic, and peripheral sent to parabolic.}
We will abuse notation and write $a$ instead of $\rho(a)$.
We observe that the notions of crossing, parallelism, and anti-parallelism only depended on the relative order of the fixed points at infinity of the hyperbolic isometries. Thus, they are independent of the choice of hyperbolic metric $\Sigma$.
Furthermore, if $a,x \in \pi_1(S)$, then $a$ crosses $x$ to the right if and only if $\ora{a}$ crosses $\ora{x}$.
In particular, in order to detect crossing, parallelism or anti-parallelism between elements, one can look at any topological realization of lifts of elements in the universal cover, and not just the geodesic ones.
Thus, $\ora{a}$ and $\ora{x}$ cross (resp. are parallel, anti-parallel), if and only if $\wt{a}$ and $\wt{x}$ cross (resp. are parallel, anti-parallel).

From Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, we get the following results.

We will use the following elementary observations about right handed crossings.

\begin{lemma}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $a$ cross $b$ to the right,
then $a^{-1}$ crosses $b^{-1}$ to the right.
\item If $a$ and $b$ are parallel, then $b^{-1}$ and $a^{-1}$ are parallel.
\item If $a$ and $b$ are parallel, then $a$ and $b^{-1}$ are anti-parallel.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

As a consequence of Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, we have the following.

\begin{lemma}
  Let $a,b,x \in \pi_1(S)$ be group elements so that $a$ and $b$ cross
  $x$ to the right. Then
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item $ab$ cross $x$ to the right.
      \item $x^{\pm 1}a$, and $ax^{\pm 1}$ cross $x$ to the right.
     \item  $ax^nbx^k$ for any $n,k \in \Z$ cross $x$ to the right.
  \end{enumerate}
    \label{lem:rhs_prod}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \begin{enumerate}
      \item Since both $a$ and $b$ cross $x$ to the right, we have the following relative cases between $a$ and $b$.
      
      \begin{enumerate}
          \item $a$ and $b$ cross to the right. In this case, by Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, part (I), we have that $ab$ crosses $x$ to the right.
          \item $b$ and $a$ cross to the right.  In this case, by Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, part (I), we also have that $ab$ crosses $x$ to the right, switching the roles of $a$ and $b$ in that figure.
          \item $a$ and $b$ are parallel.  In this case, by Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, part (II), we have that $ab$ crosses $x$ to the right.
          \item $b$ and $a$ are parallel.  In this case, by Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, part (II), we also have that $ab$ crosses $x$ to the right, switching the roles of $a$ and $b$ in that figure.
      \end{enumerate}
      \item It follows by Lemma~\ref{lem:righthandedfoundation}, part (I).
      \item It follows by combining the two previous parts.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}
