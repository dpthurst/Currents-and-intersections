\section{Intersection numbers satisfy smoothing}
\label{sec:necessary}
In this section we give a detailed proof that intersection numbers satisfy the hypotheses of our theorem. The only non-trivial condition to check is smoothing. In \cite[4.1. Examples]{MGT21:Smoothings} we gave a topological proof of this fact for curves, and then conclude the result for any geodesic current by invoking density of weighted closed curves in the space of currents.
Here we give a different proof for an arbitrary current that works in the universal cover, since it will be useful to prove later that measured laminations satisfy max-smoothing.

\begin{proposition}
Given a geodesic current $\mu$, the curve functional $i(\mu, \cdot)$ satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{mainthm:intersections}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We will check each hypothesis:
\begin{itemize}
\item (additivity and homogeneity) By definition (see \cite{Bonahon86:EndsHyperbolicManifolds}, intersection number is bilinear in both factors. In particular, additivity and homogeneity hold.
\item (stability) Observe that given a curve $C$ with hyperbolic lift $\gamma$, and $x \in \gamma$, the set of geodesics $G(x, \gamma^n x]$ can be partitioned into $\cup_{i=1}^{n-1} G(x, \gamma^{i} \cdot x]$.  Thus, by \cite[Proposition 3.14(ii)]{MZ19:PositivelyRatioed}, 
\[
i(\mu, \gamma^n) = \mu(G(x, \gamma^n x]) = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \mu(G(x, \gamma^{i} \cdot x]) = n\mu(G(x, \gamma x]) = n i(\mu, \gamma).
\]
where in the second inequality we used finite additivity of $\mu$, and in the third one, $\Gamma$-invariance.
\item (smoothing) Follows from Proposition \ref{prop:smoothing_nec} below.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

The following claim can be extracted from the proof of~\cite[Proposition~4.14]{MZ19:PositivelyRatioed}.

\begin{proposition}
Given a hyperbolic transformation $\gamma \in \pi_1(\Sigma)$, and $\mu$ any geodesic current, we have:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every $q \in \wt{\Sigma}$,
\[
\mu(G(q,\gamma \cdot q]) = \mu(X) + \mu(Y) + \mu(G(p,\gamma \cdot p]) =  \mu(X) + \mu(Y) + i(\mu, \gamma).
\]
where (compare Figure~\ref{fig:defect}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $p$ is the orthogonal projection of $q$ onto the axis of $\gamma$ (and, therefore, $\gamma \cdot p$ is the orthogonal projection of $\gamma \cdot q$ onto the axis of $\gamma$);
    \item $X$ is the set of geodesics with one endpoint on $(z, x]$ and transverse to $(q, \gamma \cdot q]$, where $z$ is the negative endpoint of the geodesic from $q$ to $\gamma \cdot q$;
    \item  and $Y$ is the set of geodesics with one endpoint on $[y, w)$ and transverse to $(q, \gamma \cdot q]$, where $w$ is the positive endpoint of the geodesic from $q$ to $\gamma \cdot q$,
\end{itemize}
\label{prop:defect}
\item  In particular, if $q \in A_{\gamma}$, then 
\[
\mu(G(q,\gamma \cdot q]) = i(\mu, \gamma).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The following proof follows similar ideas as in the proof of \cite[Theorem A]{FF21:QF}.

\begin{proposition}
Suppose $a,b \in \Gamma$ are non-trivial elements with distinct fixed points on the boundary circle $\partial \Gamma$.
Let $\mu$ be any geodesic current.
Then,
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $b$ crosses $a$ to the right, then
\[
i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b) \geq i(\mu, ab)
\]
and
\[
i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b) \geq i(\mu, ab^{-1})
\]
\item If $a$ and $b$ are $R$-parallel, then
\[
 i(\mu, ab) \geq i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b)
\]
and
\[
 i(\mu, ab) \geq i(\mu, ab^{-1})
\]
\item If $a$ and $b$ are $R$-anti-parallel, then
\[
 i(\mu, ab^{-1}) \geq i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b)
\]
and
\[
 i(\mu, ab^{-1}) \geq i(\mu, ab)
\]
\end{enumerate}
\label{prop:smoothing_nec}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We have a situation like in Figure \ref{fig:smoothing1}.
Let $p$ be the intersection point between the hyperbolic axis $A_a$ of $a$ and $A_b$ of $b$.
Since $p \in A_a$, we have $x=b^{-1} \cdot p \in A_b$.
We have $p$ and $y= a \cdot p$ are in $A_a$. Thus
\begin{align*}
i(\mu, ab) \leq & \mu(G(x, ab x]) \leq \mu(G(x, b x]) + \mu(G(bx, ab x]) = \\
&  = \mu(G(b^{-1} p, p]) + \mu(G(p, ap]) = i(\mu, b) + i(\mu, a).
\end{align*}
In the first inequality we used Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} (i), in the second, we used that 
$G(x, b x] \subset G(x, b x] \cup G(bx, ab x]$, and in the last equality we used the fact that $p$ is in the
axis of $a$ and of $b$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} (ii).
A similar argument with $ab^{-1}$ gives the other inequality.
\item We have a situation like in Figure \ref{fig:smoothing2} (note that the axes of $aB$ and $Ba$ can be in another relative position, but this will not matter).
The first inequality can be proven as follows.
Let $p$ be the intersection between $A_{ab}$ and $b \cdot A_{ab} = A_{ba}$.
Since $p \in b \cdot A_{ab}$, we have that $x = b^{-1} \cdot p$ is in $A_{ab}$ and thus $ab \cdot x \in A_{ab}$, also.
We thus have
\begin{align*}
i(\mu, ab) &= \mu(G(x, ab x]) =  \mu(G(x, p]) +  \mu(G(p, ab \cdot x]) = \\
 & =  \mu(G(b^{-1} p, p]) +  \mu(G(p, a p]) \geq i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b).
\end{align*}
In the first equality we use Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} (ii), in the second equality we used the fact that $G(x, ab x] = G(x, p] \cup G(p, ab \cdot x]$
gives a partition of $G(x, ab x]$, and $\mu$ is finitely additive. The last inequality uses again Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} (i).
For the second inequality, let $y = a^{-1} \cdot p$, which is in $A_{ba}$, since $p \in a \cdot A_{ba}=A_{ab}$.
Thus, similarly as before, we get
\begin{align*}
i(\mu, ba) &= \mu(G(y, ba y]) =  \mu(G(y, p]) +  \mu(G(p, x]) = \\
 & =  \mu(G(a^{-1} \cdot p, p]) +  \mu(G(p, b^{-1} p]) \geq \mu(G(a^{-1} \cdot p, b^{-1} p]) \geq \\
i(\mu, b^{-1} a).
\end{align*}
The only inequality that is different from the arguments above is the second. We partitioned
$G(y, ba y]$ as $G(y, p] \cup G(p, ba y]$, and then applied $b^{-1}$ to $G(p, ba y]$ to obtain $G(p,x]$.
Since $\mu$ is $\Gamma$-invariant, the third equality then holds. The fourth inequality is because
$G(a^{-1} \cdot p, b^{-1} p] \subset G(a^{-1} \cdot p, p] \cup G(p, b^{-1} p]$. The last inequality follows by 
Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} (i). This concludes the proof of the second inequality.
\item This case is the same as the second case, by changing $b$ to $b^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

We now analyze under what conditions case (ii) in the previous proposition any of the inequalities becomes an equality.


\begin{proposition}
Suppose $a,b \in \Gamma$ are non-trivial elements with distinct fixed points on the boundary circle $\partial \Gamma$. Suppose $a$ and $b$ are $R$-parallel.
Let $\mu$ be a measured lamination.
Then,
\[
 i(\mu, ab) = i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b)
\]
or
\[
 i(\mu, ab) = i(\mu, aB).
\]
\label{prop:smoothing_eq}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Following the analysis in case (ii), first inequality, we see that
\[
i(\mu, ab) = i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b)
\]
if and only if
\[
\mu(G(p, a \cdot p]) = i(\mu, a),
\]
and
\[
\mu(G(b^{-1} \cdot p, p]) = i(\mu, b).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}, the first condition is equivalent to
\[
\mu(X) + \mu(Y) = 0
\]
where $X$ is the set of geodesics with one endpoint in $(ab^+, a^+)$ and transverse to $(p, a \cdot p]$, and
$Y$ is the set of geodesics with one endpoint in $(a^-, ab^-)$ and transverse to $(p, a \cdot p]$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality}, where $X$ is depicted in purple, and $Y$ is depicted in pink.
Observe, however, that $a^{-1} \cdot X$ is the set of geodesics with an endpoint in $(ba^+, a^+)$ and transverse to $(p, a^{-1} \cdot p]$. Thus, if $\gamma \in X$, then $a^{-1} \cdot \gamma$ must intersect $\gamma$. But this is impossible, since $\mu$ is assumed to be a measured lamination.
Hence, $\mu(X)=0$.

We will assume $\mu(Y)>0$, and then show that the defect associated to $Ba$ must be $0$.
Let $\gamma \in (\supp \mu) \cap Y$.
The first thing we note is that this implies
$\mu(G(y, p]) + \mu(G(p,x]) = \mu(G(y, x])$.
Indeed, 
\[
(G(y, p]) \cup G(p,x]) - G(y,x] = G((y,p], (p,x])
\]
where the last set denotes the geodesics intersecting transversely both $(y,p]$ \emph{and} $(p,x]$. However, $\mu(G((y,p], (p,x]))=0$, since if $\eta \in G((y,p], (p,x]) \cap \supp(\mu)$, then $\eta$ would intersect $\gamma$, contradicting the fact that $\mu$ is a measured lamination.
We now consider the segment $(y,x]$, and compare its measure to the fundamental domain of $Ba$ obtained by orthogonally projecting to the axis of $Ba$, with defect sets $Z$ and $W$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality_2b1}).

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Suppose first $\gamma^+ \in (ba^+, aB^-)$, then $B \cdot \gamma^+ \in (ab^+, Ba^-)$,
    First, assume the repelling endpoint $\gamma^-$ of $\gamma$ in $(a^-, ab^-)$ is contained in $[Ba^-, ab^-)$.
    Then $B \cdot \gamma^- \in (ab^-, b^-)$.  and thus $\gamma$ and $B \cdot \gamma$ cross (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality_2d2}).
   A similar argument assuming $\gamma^-$ is in $(a^-,ba^-)$, shows that $A \cdot \gamma$ crosses $\gamma$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality_2d3}).

   It remains to consider the case $\gamma^-$ is in $(ba^-,Ba^-)$.
   This does not necessarily force any self-crossings of $\gamma$, but any $\eta$ geodesic in the defect set $W$ for $Ba$ has to intersect $\gamma, B \cdot \gamma$ or $a \cdot \gamma$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality_2d1}).

It only remains to consider the case when $\gamma^- \in (Ba^-, Ba^+)$ and $\gamma^+ \in (aB^-, aB^+)$. In this case, any $\eta$ geodesic in the defect set $W$ for $Ba$ has to intersect $\gamma, B \cdot \gamma$ or $a \cdot \gamma$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:equality_4d2}).

\end{enumerate}

If the defect of $b$ is positive, we apply the same argument to the pair $(B,A)$, with is also $R$-parallel if $(a,b)$ is.
Following the analysis above backwards shows that if $\mu(Z)>0$ or $\mu(W)>0$ implies $\mu(X)=0$ and $\mu(Y)=0$.
Now we analyze the other case in connected smoothing.
Suppose $\gamma \in W$.
If $\gamma^- \in (ba^-, ab^-)$, this forces $\gamma^+ \in (ab^+, ba^+)$. Then $A \cdot \gamma^- \in (a^-, ba^-)$ and $A \cdot \gamma^+ \in  (\gamma^+, Ab^+)$, which means $\gamma$ and $A \cdot \gamma$ intersect (Figure~\ref{fig:equality_5a}).
If $\gamma^- \in (a^-, ba^-)$, a similar analysis shows that $\gamma$ and $A \cdot \gamma$ must intersect (Figure~\ref{fig:equality_5a1}).
Suppose now $\gamma \in Z$.
Since $\gamma$ must intersect $[a \cdot p, p)$, we necessarily have $\gamma^+ \in (ab^+, ab^-)$
Then $A \cdot \gamma^- \in (a^+, ba^+)$ and $A \cdot \gamma^+ \in (ba^+, ba^-)$, and must be forward ccw compared to $\gamma^+$. Thus $\gamma$ and $A \cdot \gamma$ must intersect.
This implies that
$\mu(G[a \cdot p, p))=i(\mu, a)$.
Applying the same argument to the pair $(B,A)$, using the fact that if $a$ and $b$ are $R$-parallel, then $B$ and $A$ are $R$-parallel, shows that $\mu(G[B \cdot p, p))=i(\mu, B)$, i.e. $\mu(G[p, b \cdot p))=i(\mu, B)=i(\mu, b)$.
This shows that if $\mu$ is a measured lamination, in this situation we always have 
$i(\mu, ab)=i(\mu, a) + i(\mu, b)$.
